I am an energy and environmental policy scholar who studies how climate policy performs once it passes through the markets and bureaucracies that deliver it. I ask what a policy costs, who pays, and whether the change lasts. I am a Postdoctoral Fellow at [the Salata Institute for Climate and Sustainability](https://salatainstitute.harvard.edu/) at Harvard University, jointly appointed to the [Environment and Natural Resources Program](https://www.belfercenter.org/programs/environment-and-natural-resources) at the Harvard Kennedy School's Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs.

My research follows two lines. The first examines overlapping climate policies in U.S. transportation, where federal mandates, state clean fuel standards, and tax credits act on the same limited supply of low-carbon fuel, and asks how costs and emissions reductions are distributed across fuels, sectors, and states. The second examines delegated environmental enforcement in China, and asks how central oversight and citizen feedback shape which polluters local governments act against and whether pollution reductions persist. In both, what a policy passes through shapes the answers as much as what it says. I use equilibrium models, causal inference, and text-as-data methods.

I received my Ph.D. from the [Institute for Data, Systems, and Society](https://idss.mit.edu/) at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 2022, where my dissertation examined how misaligned incentives and information gaps undermine effective environmental governance in China. Following this, I spent 10 months as a postdoctoral associate in the [Engineering and Public Policy](https://epp.engineering.cmu.edu/) department at Carnegie Mellon University. In collaboration with Tsinghua University and Apple, I studied the effectiveness of green electricity certificates in expanding voluntary renewable energy markets and analyzed how green power trading systems influence multinational corporations' investment in clean energy.
