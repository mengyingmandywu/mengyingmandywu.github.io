excerpt: 'Three layers of climate policy apply to the same gallon of U.S. transportation fuel: a federal blending mandate with tradable credits, a federal production tax credit, and clean fuel standards in four states with banked credits and price caps. I ask what this stack costs and what one more federal dollar buys on top of it. I build a dynamic model of U.S. fuel and vehicle markets for 2026–2045 that solves the federal and state credit markets together, with investment estimated from 46 capacity decisions and credit-price expectations estimated from state credit markets. Current law abates at $184 a tonne and captures 8% of the welfare gain of a carbon price at the social cost of carbon. Extending the production credit beyond 2029 is mostly a transfer. Because the federal mandate already sets the share of biofuel in fuel, the credit lowers the price of mandate credits rather than raising that share: about three quarters of each federal dollar reaches fuel buyers as cheaper fuel, capacity barely changes, and emissions rise by 64 million tonnes as cheaper fuel raises fuel use. Whatever EPA does with the mandate in response, the extension lowers welfare. State credit markets price far less scarcity than their statutes imply, so expectations change when these effects arrive but not their size.'
